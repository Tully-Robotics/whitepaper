Tully in one page

The problem

Orchards depend on seasonal labour that is getting scarce and expensive. In the US, wages and contract labour were 40 % of expenses for fruit and tree-nut farms in 2022, against 12 % across all farms.

The answer

One robotic platform that carries task-specific tools for thinning, harvesting and pruning. Tully builds and runs the robots; the farmer buys the finished work instead of hiring crews.

The market

About €60 billion a year is spent on manual work in our crop scope. Apples alone account for €21 billion, and our obtainable market covers 462,000 ha and €4 billion in nine countries.

The machine

Eight cobot arms on four vertical rails, a multi-camera perception stack, a cutting gripper and an on-board sorting line. It is designed to harvest 15.9 t a day over 20 hours.

A €200,000 system earning about €228,000 a season at over 80 % gross margin, while charging the farmer €5,576 per hectare against €6,195 for a manual team.

The ask

€3.5 million for 24 months and two growing seasons, to prove thinning commercially and to match human tree-to-bin performance in Grade A harvesting.

01 · Tully & the tree fruit market

Every apple in the supermarket was picked by hand

The orchard labour problem

Orchards still depend on a large amount of seasonal manual labour at specific points in the production cycle. Harvesting, thinning and pruning are mainly done by hand.

That labour is getting harder to find. Western European growers have long filled their seasonal demand with workers from Poland, Romania, Hungary, Slovakia, Czechia and neighbouring countries. The European Commission states that Europe's structural demand for seasonal work is no longer fully met by EU workers, and Germany's migration authority reports declining interest among Polish and Romanian workers as conditions at home improve and agriculture competes with construction and tourism [1], [2].

It is also getting more expensive. In the United States, wages and contract labour made up 40 % of expenses for fruit and tree-nut farms in 2022, compared with 12 % across all farms [3].

A robotic, autonomous workforce

Tully aims to build the largest autonomous workforce for recurring manual work through the orchard cycle: pruning, thinning and harvesting. A shared robotic platform combines perception, manipulation, mobility and human supervision with task-specific tools, so the same technology and operations base performs several kinds of work. Tully develops and operates the robots; instead of contracting seasonal labour through agencies, the farmer works with Tully for completed agricultural outcomes.

Annual spend on manual work

In-scope crops: apples and other pome fruit, avocados, stone fruit and citrus. Obtainable market: United States, Canada, Italy, Germany, Austria, France, Belgium, Poland and Spain.

Outdoor high-value crops, apples first

Our scope is outdoor high-value crops, where delicate produce and variable growing conditions keep essential work manual: apples and other pome fruit, avocados, stone fruit and citrus. Different tasks and harvest calendars give a long operating season across crops and regions. Soft fruit, berries, outdoor field vegetables and greenhouse agriculture are out of scope.

This scope accounts for about €60 billion of annual manual labour, from 20 million hectares and 9.5 billion hours of work in a difficult outdoor environment. Citrus has the highest production volume, but apples are the biggest labour pool: of €27 billion spent on manual work in pome fruit, €21 billion is apples.

Tully's current obtainable market covers about 462,000 hectares, 235.8 million manual hours a year and a €4 billion labour pool. Commercial apple growing is concentrated in dense regional clusters: Washington produces about 68 % of US apples, and Poland, Italy, France and Germany together produce 75 % of the EU's [4]. That density lets Tully serve customers from regional hubs with short travel distances and high fleet utilisation.

The window is open now

Robotics hardware, sensors, edge computing and simulation have reached cost and performance levels that allow fast commercial development. Capable cobots now cost in the low five figures, and chip performance per dollar has risen by about 50 % in the last two years [5]. Off-road navigation stacks and autonomous mobile platforms can be bought as reliable, market-ready products.

Validated in the field and in simulation

In more than 30 conversations with growers, cooperatives, market experts and research centres, the message was the same: the market wants a reliable, automated solution for labour-intensive orchard work, whether the grower farms 1.5 ha in South Tyrol, 120 ha in Washington or runs the largest operations in Brazil.

Tully has built computer-vision pipelines and simulation environments covering sensor input, apple detection and localisation, robot approach and the picking process. The team can develop the complete perception-to-action loop and test failure cases before physical deployment. The next proof point is the first hardware prototype, to be deployed during the next thinning period.

02 · Vision, company & business model

Selling finished orchard work, not machines

Building a global robotic workforce

Tully's vision is to build and operate the largest robotic workforce for outdoor high-value crops. The team combines computer vision, robot systems, deployment and mechanical engineering to build a system that finishes thinning, harvesting and pruning autonomously. Off-the-shelf components are used where possible; parts like the gripper are developed in-house. The software stack that finishes the orchard work is Tully's own, while navigation can come from partners.

Regional teams deploy, service and maintain the machines, and remote teams supervise the fleet for real-time recovery. Working across regional crops and both hemispheres allows operations all year and high utilisation.

Outcome as a service

Today, farmers run the whole agricultural process and also recruit seasonal labour: agencies, accommodation, meals, schedules, absences and daily supervision. With Tully they get thinning, harvesting or pruning as a service. Tully takes care of machine operation, supervision and scheduling; the farmer keeps their seasonal planning and gains predictability, accountability and operational security. Machine work also improves quality and uniformity over manual labour.

Targeting the labour budget

The labour budget is much larger than the machine budget. An Italian farmer with 4 ha buys a mobile lift for €80,000–120,000 that lasts more than ten years. The same 4 ha need about 2,600 hours of manual work, costing about $40,000 every year.

That is one reason Tully sells the recurring work instead of the machine. Running the full operation in the early stages also gives faster iteration, close contact with farmers and regional markets, and an easier path for farmers to adopt the technology.

Scaling through regional hubs

Tully expands region by region through hubs that combine local sales and operations with fleet deployment, maintenance, spare parts and logistics. Each hub grows outward from a concentration of customers, raising fleet utilisation while cutting travel, recovery and service times. Every deployment adds field data and experience, which improves performance and unit economics and makes the next deployment easier to win and serve.

03 · Unit economics & potential for growth

Twice the daily output of a picking team, at a lower cost per hectare

The manual benchmark

The benchmark is the complete tree-to-bin workflow: finding and picking apples, preserving fruit quality, moving fruit into bins and handling the interruptions that keep harvesting going. We measure accepted kilograms per working hour and per day, days to harvest a hectare, and the fully loaded cost per hectare, including wages, recruitment, accommodation, transport and supervision.

The standard today is a mobile lift with conveyor belts, such as the Munckhof Pluk-o-Trak, which needs six workers for full use. In South Tyrol harvesting with such a machine achieves 700 kg/h, 7 t a day and 7.4 days per hectare, at €840 a day.

The robotic threshold

Tully's commercial target is to match human tree-to-bin output per hour at the same quality and a lower cost, sustained through the work day after failed and damaged attempts, repositioning, bin changes, charging and downtime. Because a robot can run about 20 hours a day instead of a worker's 10, supervision, remote support, maintenance, energy and logistics are part of the operating model.

Human performance equals 5.6 s per apple per person. Research projects have reached this speed per robotic arm with acceptable damage: a team from Zhejiang University of Technology reported 4.8 s per arm, and Michigan State University 5.9 s [6], [7].

04 · Competition & reasons for failure

Picking an apple is solved. Picking every day, all season, is not.

From feasibility to commercialisation

Research and field trials have already shown the core capabilities: detecting fruit, planning a path across a tree, approaching the fruit and picking it. The commercial challenge is to sustain that performance through a working day and a whole season, across changing orchard conditions. That takes robust machines, reliable fruit handling, fast recovery from failures, and an organisation that can deploy, supervise and maintain a fleet at a competitive cost.

Lessons from earlier attempts

Abundant Robotics combined computer vision, vacuum picking and fruit conveyance in a dedicated harvesting machine. Despite showing that it could pick apples, the company closed in 2021 after insufficient commercial traction and unsuccessful fundraising. Its successor described the inherited prototype as over-engineered and expensive to produce: simplicity and fast iteration matter [8], [9].

Agrobot built a large strawberry-harvesting platform with up to 24 independent picking arms, fruit perception and selective stem cutting. Tully's interview with its founder highlighted how hard it is to develop manipulation, navigation, the vehicle and outdoor reliability at the same time. Capable picking technology needs a manageable development scope and a credible route into commercial operations.

Commercialisation alongside development

Tully's roadmap ties growing autonomy to progressively broader customer services. We commercialise useful capabilities during development, starting with one-arm thinning, and expand as field performance and operating economics are proven, towards a fully autonomous multi-robot system for the whole orchard logistics chain. We use off-the-shelf components where they fit and build specialised hardware where the task requires it. Iteration speed is a proxy for learning and has to be as high as possible.

Competing approaches today

Companies building harvesting robots for tree fruit use integrated ground machines or flying picking robots. They differ in how they reach fruit, handle it after picking and fit into the farmer's workflow.

05 · Technical concept

Follow one apple through the machine

The autonomous harvesting system is the foundation of an autonomous orchard workforce. At its core is an autonomous mobile platform with eight robotic arms, visual perception pipelines and an internal fruit-handling line.

Modularity is a central design principle: one platform performs several tasks (harvesting, thinning and pruning) across several crops (apples, pears and citrus) instead of needing a single-purpose machine for each. Robust middleware and standardised communication protocols support this. Commercial off-the-shelf components, wherever they exist, keep R&D spending and capital risk low and shorten the path to the field.

This chapter describes harvesting, the technically harder task, even though thinning comes first in development: the thinning tools are a sub-system of the same machine, not a different one.

      Technical details

      Software architecture and compute

      Three principles govern the software. First, all perception pipelines write into a single shared state model, which holds tracked fruit with stable IDs, grades and approach vectors alongside the canopy occupancy grid; one global scheduler owns task allocation. Second, each robotic arm runs independently on its own real-time node for inverse kinematics, trajectory generation, visual servoing and grasp state, and completes its assigned targets without further negotiation. Third, the system is fault-tolerant: if an arm hits a fault, such as an unreachable target, the machine keeps working at a reduced rate and its work zones are redistributed.

      Control and computation run on a hybrid architecture: an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin and a Beckhoff CX7000 PLC. AI inference, neural vision pipelines and global motion planning need the parallel compute of the GPU, which cannot guarantee non-preemptible determinism. The PLC runs the deterministic real-time control loops, enforces safety-rated interlocks and keeps actuation reliable independently of the AI stack.

      Stages communicate by publish/subscribe over bounded queues and share no state. Several loops run at their own rates, so no stage waits on another. Because every stage already publishes to the bus, observability and data capture are just another subscriber: telemetry, a black-box recorder for offline replay, GNSS-tagged yield and grade data per row and bin for the grower, and automatic capture of hard cases for retraining.

      Navigation and the base

      Each harvesting unit consists of a master platform, which picks, and up to two autonomous slave units that carry the bins: one for quality apples and one for industrial grade, each with a gross capacity of 300 kg in standard euro-pallet format. Both must handle unpaved terrain, lateral slopes, changing soil friction and variable weather, conditions indoor vehicles never face.

      The first solution uses a platform from Frucotec, developed for apple orchards; the team has offered full access to and support for the vehicle. Autonomous navigation comes from Nature Robots, whose lightweight solution for vineyards and orchards includes industrial-grade compute, RTK-GNSS and LiDAR [10]. Other platforms and navigation systems will be benchmarked too. The footprint slightly exceeds a euro pallet for compatibility with standard bulk bins and freight, while staying manoeuvrable in high-density orchards. Field-exposed components are rated IP65 or better.

      For reach across the whole canopy, each side of the harvester has two 2.5 m vertical rails, each carrying two articulated arms: eight manipulators in total. The rails move the arms vertically; with the arm reach, the system covers the full height of standard 3 m trees.

      Camera stack

      Besides the cameras in the hands, the machine carries eight perception cameras: six colour and two depth cameras, on one slim mast at each leading corner. The gap between the machine and the leaves is only about 0.45 m, so all cameras are yawed about 30° outward from the driving direction. The line of sight to the canopy grows to about 0.9 m, the arms and columns stay out of view, and each apple passes through every camera's view from several angles as the machine advances.

      Perception pipeline and target selection

      The perception and control stack is a sequence of layers, pipelined so the acquisition layer already works on the next frame while planning works on the current world state. Frames from all cameras are normalised and batched into one tensor for a real-time instance-segmentation model served on NVIDIA Triton, which separates apples, stems, branches, trunks and leaves. Branches feed collision avoidance; stems feed orientation.

      Everything below segmentation writes into the state model: a registry of tracked apples, each with a stable ID, a filtered 3D position and accumulated characteristics. Combining views over a short time gives much better diameter and occlusion estimates than any single frame. Colour is measured in the CIELAB a* channel after masking highlights, shadows and leaves; size comes from RANSAC sphere fits on depth; orientation from stem segmentation, ellipse fitting and the calyx depression. Candidate approach vectors are sampled over a hemisphere around each fruit and checked by ray-casting a gripper-sized cylinder through the occupancy grid.

      Each tracked apple then passes a set of gates before it is picked: a confirmed track, colour and size against the EU marketing standard for the target class, visible defects, stem visibility, reach, a clear approach corridor, cluster order and time left in the arm's zone. The outcome is one of four: pick for the quality bin, pick for industrial, leave for the next pass, or skip. Among pickable fruit the planner ranks by expected value per second and re-plans every cycle.

      Pick cycle and motion control

      Inverse kinematics uses the redundant seven-axis chain (six arm joints plus the rail) to keep the elbow away from the canopy and the joints away from their limits. Jerk-limited, time-optimal trajectories keep the platform and cameras steady, and an occupancy grid built from depth frames and labelled with branches and trunks protects the arm from structural obstacles.

      Open-loop motion from long-range observations is prone to estimation errors, so the final approach switches to image-based visual servoing. A wrist-mounted camera tracks the target; a lightweight network finds the fruit's centre and apparent radius in a cropped region, and the controller drives the offset and radius error to zero at 30–60 Hz, streaming Cartesian velocity commands to the arm.

      Gripper

      Commercial agricultural end-effectors have clear limits, so Tully is developing a specialised multi-finger gripper. It has three tendon-driven gripping fingers and a central cutting finger with a guarded scissor. Each gripping finger carries a miniature RGB camera that checks the colour of the hidden back of the apple; if it fails, the apple is released and logged for a later pass. A 3D camera and lights at the centre enable visual servoing and night work.

      Manual harvesting relies on a two-handed twist. The robot grasps and cuts in one motion instead, which prevents stem pull-out and allows an immediate hand-over to transport. Cutting also removes rigid protruding stems, as in 'Fuji', that puncture neighbouring fruit in bulk bins, which cooperatives reward financially.

      Intra-system fruit transport and sorting

      After detachment, fruit drops into a flexible polymer tube beneath the end-effector and descends under control to a central buffer with an active bottom belt. From there a conveyor feeds an inline sorter, a miniature version of a packinghouse grader: a multi-camera array images each apple through 360° and finds defects a few millimetres across, including on the hidden rear. Correct grading avoids heavy financial penalties; faulty apples go to industrial grade, which is also paid.

      Premium apples are lowered into the bulk bin by a vertical conveyor whose height follows the fill level, preventing impact damage. Industrial apples go directly into a side container, where minor pressure marks do not change their value.

      Master, slaves and the hive

      When a bin reaches its 300 kg limit, the system decouples itself: the master's lowering mechanism retracts clear of the bin, the slave drives autonomously to a staging area, unloads the full bin, takes an empty one and docks again without manual intervention. Meanwhile harvesting continues: the master shifts toward the secondary grade and its conveyor lines buffer the fruit picked in between. The buffer is sized for the average transit and docking time, so bin exchange does not stall the arms.

      Fleet units stay synchronised over a wireless link, and the follow-me mode uses a physical lock-in. An eSIM uplink sends data to the Tully cloud and gives the farmer a live view; harvesting does not depend on it.

      User experience

      Farmers order the service in the browser. During onboarding they record their crops, cooperative and orchard area, and draw the geofenced boundaries of their blocks on a map, so the system knows exactly where to operate and navigation needs no manual setup in the field.

      The farmer never operates the robot. Tully personnel handle deployment, positioning and start-up on site, and a central remote team monitors every machine. Farmers book and manage service appointments, review farm profiles and follow harvesting progress, yields and quality in real time on the Tully cloud platform. Safety protocols let farm workers and operators work close to the machines.

      06 · Roadmap & go-to-market

      Four years, paced by the apple season

      Apple farming runs on an annual biological cycle that sets the pace of development and sales. The modular system, built on the unified ROS middleware, lets sub-systems be developed and versioned in parallel and lets the machine switch quickly between seasons.

      Harvesting is the most valuable task to automate and also the hardest. We start with manual thinning, removing excess immature fruitlets, because it gets a commercial solution to farmers fastest. Nearly all components carry over to the harvester, which becomes an upgrade rather than a new machine. Thinning also lets us shape orchards for robotic pickability as well as yield.

      Each year has three parts:

      Off-season, from the end of harvest to the end of blossom: about six months of focused development, new features and technical debt.
      Thinning, from early fruit growth until fruitlets reach 25–30 mm, usually in June: real-world thinning and any tests that need trees in leaf.
      Harvesting, from June until the harvest window closes, two to three months: the most important phase, for harvesting and data collection.

      Farmers have offered their orchards for testing, as has the research institute Laimburg with its dedicated research orchards.

      Year 1: autonomous thinning prototype

      The goal for year one is a full prototype that can thin at the end of the thinning period, and a year of data collection, especially during harvest. Once funding arrives, the hardware is bought and integrated: the Frucotec base gets autonomous driving modules and is tested in orchards, the arms are mounted on the columns to detect and cut apples, and the thinning gripper prototypes are developed and tested. The AI models, most of which already exist, are improved in the lab on mock walls all year.

      After blossom, all components come together in two mirrored systems tested in the field, for continuous operation and redundancy. As canopy and fruit grow, the system is calibrated and benchmarked against changing conditions, producing large amounts of data and test cases.

      In parallel, a small team mounts a robot arm on a Pluk-o-Trak in place of a human picker as a harvesting baseline. This bypasses autonomous navigation and fruit transport and isolates the perception-to-action loop under real conditions; success opens a semi-automated harvesting service for pilot customers the following year. The ML team captures orchard data across weather, lighting and night, plus 360° per-apple images from cooperative packinghouse sorters. At the end of the season, all components come together for a fully autonomous harvesting baseline at Laimburg. The seed round follows, based on these results.

      Year 2: professional autonomous thinning

      The thinning prototype becomes a professional machine used commercially in a dedicated region. In the off-season, the robot and gripper are hardened for field reliability and durability; because Tully operates the machines itself, it keeps full control of ongoing refinements.

      A go-to-market team launches the thinning service in South Tyrol through live demonstrations at trade shows, cooperatives and farmers' associations such as the Bauernverband and the Beratungsring; farmers book and pay through the Tully website. Machines are built to match the bookings by a small manufacturing team. Every deployment has at least one core engineer on site, and manual labour stays on call as a contingency.

      A targeted, unadvertised service retrofits customer-owned Pluk-o-Traks with standardised arm assemblies for selected pilot partners: an intermediate tier before the full autonomous fleet, and live validation for the harvesting system.

      Year 3: autonomous harvesting and regional hubs

      Year three builds the fully autonomous harvester and professionalises thinning through regional hubs in South Tyrol, Bodensee and Washington, each with its own staff, workshop, vehicle storage, spare parts and fleet monitoring, supported by a Series A round. Engineering shifts to harvesting: autonomous slave units shuttle bins to the headlands over a farm-wide wireless link, and the master gains the fruit transport and sorting system. The harvest season brings the international launch of autonomous harvesting with pilot partners.

      Year 4: global launch

      With verified demand for thinning and harvesting, a Series B round funds new hubs worldwide. The same platform runs both seasons, with thinning grippers in spring and harvesting effectors in autumn, and the autonomous harvester launches commercially in every hub region.

      08 · Investment

      Capital to commercialisation

      €3.5M

      for 24 months and two growing seasons

      Two growing seasons to build the first autonomous orchard robot with proven thinning and harvesting performance, and to bring the first services into commercial trials with farmers.

      24-month spending allocation

      Most capital goes to the team that develops, integrates and deploys the robot. Hardware covers arms, platforms, gripper development, prototyping and field testing; the rest covers external services, certification, software, tools and company infrastructure.

      What this round proves

      By the end of 24 months, Tully will have a first version of its autonomous orchard robot with proven performance in thinning and harvesting. Thinning and industrial-apple harvesting will have completed commercial trials. For Grade A apples, the system will have shown it can match human tree-to-bin performance in output and fruit quality.

      The two seasons also establish the operating requirements behind that performance: supervision, maintenance, recovery, logistics and delivery cost. These results are the basis for expanding commercial deployments and financing the next fleet.

      Future and outlook

      As European planting moves from 3 m row spacing toward narrower 2 m systems with two-dimensional fruiting walls, a lean chassis keeps soil compaction low and allows tight headland turns, even on slopes.
